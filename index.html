About

Questions I keep coming back to

  • What changes when the incentives change?
  • Who pays when a neighborhood changes?
  • How do people decide about money when they only have part of the picture?
  • What would make a hard dataset useful to someone who is not an analyst?

I am most interested in decisions that look simple from the outside and turn out to depend on incentives: why a family moves, how a block changes, what a household does with its savings, what a policy actually rewards.

Economics gives me a way to think about those questions, and data analysis gives me a way to check my thinking. Finance and public policy are where the ideas meet real choices with real costs. I like projects that end in something people can use, not only a conclusion.

These fields overlap more than their names suggest, so I treat them as one set of questions, not separate career paths.

Research

Housing pressure in the Atlanta region

An independent project on gentrification and the policy tools that can slow it.

Independent research · Ongoing · Paper dated September 2026

Reclaiming Urban Belonging: Policy Interventions to Reverse Gentrification in Atlanta

The question

Where is gentrification pushing residents out in the Atlanta region, and which policy tools can reduce it in a state that does not allow rent control? Gentrification follows public decisions about where to build trails, transit, and roads, and it moves faster than policy can respond, so the earlier a community acts, the less it costs to keep a home affordable.

Data and methods

The paper combines public housing data, interviews with local officials and housing organizations, and case studies of three cities that fought displacement with different tools: Burlington, Vermont (community land trusts), Washington, D.C. (tenant purchase rights), and Minneapolis (zoning reform).

My first phase's files were lost, so in fall 2026 I rebuilt the analysis from the public datasets below, covering the 11-county region of the Atlanta Regional Commission (ARC), with a closer look at Forsyth County. The plan is to map each measure by census tract, find tracts where several pressures overlap, and test them against ARC's record of which tracts changed between 2000 and 2019.

Data: American Community Survey measures (home values, rents, rent burden, renter share, income), ARC permit, eviction, transit, and neighborhood-change data, and ARC household forecasts to 2035.

What I found

  • Prices move at the announcement. Homes within a half-mile of the BeltLine rose 17.9% to 26.6% more in value than comparable homes between 2011 and 2015, with premiums appearing years before the trails were built.Immergluck and Balan (2018); Immergluck (2009)
  • Policy has not kept pace. By spring 2022, Atlanta's inclusionary zoning had produced 138 completed affordable units, with 646 more underway or planned, while the city loses more than 1,500 affordable units a year.Atlanta Civic Circle (2022)
  • The pressure moves outward. ARC forecasts about 350,000 new households across its 11 counties by 2035, about 42,000 of them in Forsyth County.ARC and HR&A Advisors (2026)
  • Georgia limits the toolbox. State law bars local governments from regulating rent on private property, so protection has to come through land, zoning, and development agreements.O.C.G.A. 44-7-19

The county-level results from my first phase were lost, so the paper reports the rebuilt framework. Forecasts are forecasts, and the BeltLine research shows an association with price increases, not every cause.

What I recommend

  1. Act at the announcement of a major trail, transit line, or interchange, before prices move.
  2. Scale community land trusts on public land.
  3. Extend inclusionary requirements to where public investment is going.
  4. Allow more homes near jobs and transit.
  5. Build an early warning system that combines data with resident input. This is the purpose of the toolkit below.
  6. Study a tenant right of first refusal, which would require new legislation in Georgia.

My role

I carried out the research on my own: assembling and rebuilding the data, interviewing district commissioners and housing nonprofits, comparing three other cities, and writing the paper. I then built the toolkit so residents can use the work.

Toolkit

Atlanta Region Housing Pressure Toolkit

I built the toolkit as a live website so residents can use my research. The community guide is its printable paper version, for people without easy access to technology.

The problem

Rising rents and new development can push long-time residents out of their neighborhoods, often before anyone notices. The data that shows it is scattered and written for analysts, and residents often do not know their rights or where to find help.

Who it is for

Residents, community organizations, and local officials. I have presented it at town halls and to Forsyth County Commissioner Alfred John.

The toolkit (live website)

  • Covers Atlanta's 11-county region
  • Maps 14 housing and economic indicators
  • Flags early signs of displacement risk
  • Compares long-term trends and projected growth
  • Lets residents report neighborhood changes without names or personal information
  • Works in English and Spanish

The community guide (paper handout)

  • A paper version of the toolkit for people who cannot use the website
  • A checklist of warning signs
  • What the housing numbers mean
  • Renters' rights in Georgia
  • Tools for homeowners
  • Free legal help and Atlanta housing nonprofits

What the data shows across the 11 counties: from 2010 to 2024, the share of all households under cost strain fell from 39.1% to 33.8%, while the share of renters under cost strain rose from 51.5% to 55.0%. After inflation, median household income fell in 5 of the 11 counties. Source: U.S. Census Bureau data compiled by the Atlanta Regional Commission.
